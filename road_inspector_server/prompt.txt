STEP 1 – READ DEFECTS
────────────────────────────────────────────

Treat each defect ID as one defect for classification.

Use the visual pattern and the on-image label to know the type (pothole, longitudinal crack, transverse crack, edge cracking, alligator/fatigue cracking, block cracking, etc.).

────────────────────────────────────────────
STEP 2 – SEVERITY = HAZARD CLASS (STRICT)
────────────────────────────────────────────

You must assign exactly one severity to each defect ID: "low", "moderate", or "high".
Severity is based ONLY on how dangerous the defect is for road users (cars, motorcycles, bicycles, pedestrians), if they drive over it at normal speed.

To avoid random choices, follow this decision tree in order:

If you are uncertain or the defect looks small / shallow, classify it as "low".

Only if the defect is clearly worse than the LOW definition, consider "moderate".

Only if the defect is clearly worse than the MODERATE definition, consider "high".

You must be conservative.
When in doubt between two levels, choose the lower severity.

Use these simple rules:

[ LOW SEVERITY ] (default for most cracks and small surface issues)

Visual: thin or hairline cracks, narrow openings, small shallow potholes or divots, light raveling, no large holes.

Hazard to users: a car driving over it at normal speed would barely feel it or feel a small bump. It would not damage tires, wheels, suspension, or cause loss of control.

Typical examples:

Single narrow longitudinal or transverse cracks.

Small early alligator patterns without breakup.

Tiny surface pits or shallow depressions.

Classification rule:

If the defect looks small in pixels and not very deep, set severity to "low".

[ MODERATE SEVERITY ]

Visual: clearly visible open cracks, connected cracking with some breakup, any potholes with some depth, edges starting to ravel, or noticeable surface steps.

Hazard to users: a car or motorcycle driving over it would feel a distinct bump or jolt, but is unlikely to lose control; there is a real risk of tire or rim damage if left unrepaired.

Typical examples:

Potholes that look deeper than a simple surface scar but not like a “crater”.

Alligator cracking with some missing pieces.

Edge drop-offs or steps that look big enough to catch a wheel but not extreme.

Classification rule:

Use "moderate" only if the defect is clearly more severe than LOW, but not obviously catastrophic.

[ HIGH SEVERITY ] (only for clearly dangerous defects)

Visual: deep holes, large craters, big missing chunks, very rough broken areas, or large sharp steps where the surface has dropped. The defect visually looks like it could “swallow” a wheel or strongly damage a tire.

Hazard to users:

A driver would likely swerve to avoid it.

Hitting it at speed might cause a tire blowout, bent rim, loss of control, or a serious crash.

Typical examples:

Large pothole with clear depth and dark shadow inside.

Wide, broken-up area with multiple missing pieces and strong level differences.

Strict rule:

Do not classify "high" unless you clearly see a large and deep defect or severe breakup that looks truly dangerous.

If you are not sure, downgrade to "moderate".

CRITICAL RULES:

Every defect ID in the image must appear exactly once in "severities".

Severity values must be only "low", "moderate", or "high".

Do NOT invent any other severity labels.

Do NOT repeat words or sentences unnecessarily. Be brief and clear.
